The exam

L5 Data Center is Arista's ACE Professional – Data Center credential. You can take the two Specialist exams (Operations AN-DC-OP01 and Engineering AN-DC-EN01) and get the Professional badge automatically, or sit the single Professional exam AN-DC-PR01, which mixes both. Each is a timed, proctored, open-book lab. You get a scenario in the same virtual environment the Arista Academy labs use: EOS switches with a CloudVision instance, and a list of tasks to build, fix, and verify. Arista does not publish weights, task counts, or the pass mark.

That shapes how to study. You are graded on the end state of the devices, not on what you can recite. Speed at the CLI, a habit of verifying every task, and knowing where to look things up matter more than memorized trivia.

How to sit a hands-on exam

  • Read every task before typing. Note dependencies (an EVPN task fails if the underlay task before it is wrong).
  • Draw the topology and fill in ASNs, loopbacks, VNIs and VRFs on scratch paper in the first ten minutes.
  • Make changes in a configure session so you can review the diff and abort a mistake.
  • Verify each task with its show command before moving on. Then write memory.
  • Never break management access or the CloudVision connection (TerminAttr, Management1, the MGMT VRF).
  • If a task asks for CloudVision, do it in CloudVision. A correct config pushed from the CLI shows up as out of compliance and may not score.
  • Skip a stuck task and come back. Leave 20 minutes at the end to re-verify everything.

Open book, used well

  • The EOS User Manual is searchable. Practise finding a feature's config chapter in under a minute.
  • On the switch, ? and show running-config all | section <feature> show every option and its default.
  • show running-config | section router bgp and show active (inside a mode) keep you oriented.
  • This guide's show-command sheet is meant to be open beside the exam.
  • Check the current exam policies on training.arista.com for what materials are allowed.

Hardware foundations: fabric speed, oversubscription and the product line

Background for D4/D5 design questions and for reading datasheets in the real world. Not hands-on exam tasks. Based on Arista Warrior ch. 5, "Fabric Speed" (pp.26–27), and ch. 6, "Arista Products" (pp.28, 30–33), with added design math.

Fabric speed and nonblocking

Know

  • Fabric = the paths that connect every port to every other port. In a fixed (top-of-rack) switch it's inside the ASIC(s); in a chassis there is a fabric on each line card and a master fabric (the "backplane", now usually hot-swap fabric modules at the back) joining the cards. AW 5 p.26
  • Nonblocking = every port can send and receive at line rate, all at the same time. Most home and office switches block, and that's fine for them; data center fabrics usually shouldn't. AW 5 p.26
  • Count both directions. Ethernet is full duplex, so 10 ports × 10G needs 200G of fabric to be nonblocking. A "100G fabric" on that switch is 2:1 oversubscribed. A common spec-sheet trick is a fabric number equal to the sum of the ports, quoted as if it meant nonblocking. AW 5 p.26
  • Each ASIC is a choke point. If several ports share an ASIC (or uplink) slower than their total, those ports are oversubscribed even when the "fabric" figure looks large. Some chassis cards did this on purpose: groups of ports sharing one port's bandwidth, with a "dedicated" mode giving one port full speed. Fine for servers, not for inter-switch links. AW 5 p.26–27
  • Cascaded ASICs (small ASICs feeding a bigger one) give connectivity, but aggregate throughput is capped by the middle ASIC, and the design doesn't scale past a box or two. Meshing big ASICs in the middle of a chassis does scale, if the center ASICs can carry the total. AW 5 p.27
  • Single ASIC vs many: a 7050S-64 switched 64×10G nonblocking on one Broadcom Trident+ ASIC. A 7500E 48×10G line card used one ASIC per 8 ports (6 per card) with 1.25 Tbps to the fabric per card: still nonblocking, with a tiny extra latency when traffic crosses ASICs, which only matters for things like high-frequency trading. AW 5 p.27

Reading a datasheet

  • Throughput (Tbps): add up port speeds. Check whether the figure counts one direction or both: chip vendors usually quote one direction (a "12.8T" chip is 32×400G), system datasheets often double it ("25.6 Tbps").
  • Forwarding rate (pps): line rate at minimum-size frames. A 64-byte frame plus 20 bytes of preamble and gap is 84 bytes = 672 bits, so 10G = 14.88 Mpps and 100G = 148.8 Mpps. Multiply by ports and compare with the datasheet's Bpps; if the Bpps is lower, the box can't do line rate with small packets.
  • Buffers: shallow shared on-chip buffers (Trident/Tomahawk class: 7050X, 7060X) suit low-latency leaf and spine roles. Deep buffers with virtual output queues (Jericho class: 7280R, 7500R) absorb incast and speed mismatches and carry internet-scale routes (FlexRoute, AW 20). See AW ch. 3–4.
  • Table sizes: MAC, host routes, LPM routes, ACL/TCAM entries. In EVPN fabrics, host-route (/32) scale on leaves is the one that bites.

Oversubscription in a leaf-spine fabric

Ratios and sizing

  • Leaf ratio = server-facing bandwidth ÷ uplink bandwidth. 48×25G (1.2T) with 4×100G up (400G) is 3:1; with 8×100G (800G) it's 1.5:1; 48×10G with 6×40G is 2:1. The switch itself is nonblocking either way: the "oversubscription" is a design choice in how many ports you use for uplinks.
  • Typical targets: 3:1 or better for general compute, 1:1 for storage, AI/ML and east-west heavy racks. Lower ratios cost spine ports.
  • Spine count = leaf uplinks (one link per spine). It also sets ECMP width: with 4 spines the leaf needs maximum-paths 4 (5.2). Losing one of 4 spines removes 25% of fabric bandwidth; one of 2 removes 50%.
  • Leaf count ≤ spine port count (one port per leaf per spine). Beyond that, add a super-spine tier or bigger spines.
  • MLAG pairs count as two leaves for spine ports but one ASN in the eBGP design (4.1, 5.3).
  • Hashing is per flow, so one big flow can't use more than one link. Equal-size links and good entropy (VXLAN puts an inner-flow hash in the outer UDP source port) keep ECMP even.

Spec-sheet and design traps

  • A fabric number equal to the port total (not double) means 2:1 if the vendor counts both directions. Do the math yourself. AW 5 p.26
  • "Wire-speed" ports that share an ASIC or a slot connection: read the per-module and per-ASIC numbers, not only the system total. AW 5 p.26–27
  • Forwarding rate quoted at large packets only. Ask for 64-byte performance.
  • Higher model number isn't always newer or better: the 7150 did things the 7160 can't. Pick by port density and speed first, then features (ASIC-dependent), then price. AW 6 p.28

Physical details worth knowing

  • Front = the side with the network ports. AW 6 p.28

Features and software

  • Nonblocking: Arista sells nonblocking switches, even the small 7010T. Where you see oversubscription in an Arista design, it's a ratio you chose with uplinks. AW 6 p.31
  • FlexRoute (R-series): full internet table in hardware. See 3.4 and AW ch. 20. AW 6 p.31
  • AlgoMatch: a replacement for TCAM lookups on some models: faster and with more room for very large or numerous ACLs. Hardware-dependent. AW 6 p.31
  • Optics: like other big vendors, Arista switches reject third-party optics by default. That's partly vendor lock and partly protection against bad or mis-coded optics, which cause real TAC cases. SFP+ replaced GBIC and XenPak; QSFP+ and SFP+ both take twinax (DAC) cables for cheap short runs. AW 6 p.31–32
  • One EOS image for every platform: no feature sets or release trains, just one binary and a minimum version per model. That's why upgrades are simple (D1.2). AW 6 p.32
  • vEOS (lab and router), AnyCloud (now CloudEOS: EOS in AWS/Azure linked to your network with VPN), cEOS (container). See R5. AW 6 p.32
  • CloudVision: CVP is the GUI platform for config management, change control and telemetry (on-prem cluster or CVaaS; D2). CVX is EOS on a server that switches share state with (a master Sysdb), used for the VXLAN control service (6.1). AW 6 p.32

Practice: hardware

A 24 × 10G switch advertises a 240 Gbps fabric. Is it nonblocking?

Not if 240G is the total for both directions: nonblocking needs 24 × 10G × 2 = 480G. Unless the vendor says 240G is per direction, it's 2:1.

Leaves have 48 × 25G server ports and 6 × 100G uplinks to 6 spines. What's the oversubscription ratio, and what's the ECMP setting?

1200G ÷ 600G = 2:1. Six equal-cost paths, so maximum-paths 6 ecmp 6 (or more).

A new switch arrives with blue-handled fans and PSUs for a rack with front-to-back airflow. Problem?

Yes. Blue is back-to-front, so it would breathe hot-aisle air. Order red (front-to-back, -F) units, and never mix colors in one switch.

EOS Management

Changing a running switch safely, upgrading it without an outage, and getting it back when it won't boot. EOS keeps all state in SysDB and runs each feature as a separate agent, which is why an agent can restart without a reload and why config sessions and rollback work so well.

1.1

EOS foundations: SysDB, the CLI, and safe config change

One unit covering Arista Warrior ch. 7 (SysDB), ch. 8 (Introduction to EOS) ch. 9 (Configuration Management) and ch. 12 (Bash). Read it in that order: how EOS keeps state, how you drive it from the CLI, how you change it safely, and the Linux underneath. Book pages are cited; extra detail comes from current EOS docs.

Know

  • SysDB is the in-memory database at the center of EOS. Every feature runs as its own Linux process (an agent: Stp, Bgp, Lldp, ConfigAgent…) in protected memory, and agents never talk to each other directly: they publish state to SysDB and subscribe to what they need. AW 7 p.35
  • If an agent crashes, ProcMgr restarts it, it reads its last state back from SysDB, and carries on without reconverging. One bad feature doesn't take the box down, unlike a monolithic OS where one crashed routine crashes everything. AW 7 p.35–36
  • The running-config is a view of SysDB: ConfigAgent writes your CLI changes into SysDB and renders show running-config from it. SysDB lives in RAM and is rebuilt from startup-config at every boot. AW 7 p.37
  • EOS is Linux. bash drops you to a shell; FastCli -p 15 -c "cmd" runs CLI from bash.
  • Config sessions stage changes, show a diff, and commit atomically. commit timer rolls back unless you confirm.
  • Checkpoints save a named copy of the running config; configure replace swaps the whole running config for another file and applies only the differences.
  • Maintenance mode drains traffic from a switch before work: BGP sends a lower preference or withdraws, MLAG and interfaces can be quiesced.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting write memory. A reload or a CloudVision reconcile will wipe unsaved work.
  • Leaving a session uncommitted. Changes in a session do nothing until commit.
  • commit timer without the confirm: the change silently rolls back when the timer ends.
  • configure replace or checkpoint restore expecting a merge: everything not in the file is removed. To add a snippet, use copy flash:file running-config. AW 9 p.45, 47
  • Editing running-config directly while a session is open, then committing the session: your direct edits disappear. AW 9 p.48
  • show run | include bgp misses "BGP" and loses context. Use | section. Stacked | include … | include … doesn't filter twice; use grep. AW 8 p.41, 44–45
  • No duplex, no interface range, no do, no service password-encryption. AW 8 p.39–41
  • AAA without local fallback, or with the server in the wrong VRF, locks everyone out. Test from a second session before you log out.
  • configure replace with a file that lacks management config drops your access.
  • An agent's uptime shorter than the switch's uptime means it restarted. Check show agent logs crash before blaming the network.
  • Never kill Sysdb or use agent X terminate on a production box: killing Sysdb is a reboot without the "unsaved changes" prompt. AW 7 p.36–37

SysDB in depth: why it matters

  • Publish/subscribe: each agent mounts the parts of SysDB it cares about. Example: you configure a BGP neighbor, ConfigAgent writes it, the Bgp agent sees the change and brings up the session, learned routes go to SysDB, and the routing and hardware agents (for example SandL3Unicast or StrataL3) program the ASIC. No agent calls another.
  • Hitless restarts: because state survives in SysDB, an agent can restart without dropping traffic. The same design lets EOS upgrade or restart pieces of itself: SSU (smart system upgrade, fixed switches keep forwarding while the CPU reloads), and MLAG ISSU, where one peer at a time is upgraded while the other forwards (1.2, 4.1).
  • One source of truth for every interface: CLI, eAPI, gNMI/OpenConfig, SNMP and CloudVision all read and write the same SysDB state, so they always agree. eAPI's JSON keys are SysDB's view; that's why | json is reliable (7.3).
  • Streaming telemetry: TerminAttr subscribes to SysDB (and the routing state) and streams every change to CloudVision, which keeps the history used in CloudVision's time-series views and snapshots (D2). NetDB is the idea of extending this state network-wide; CVX (ch. 6, 21) is an early form, a central EOS that switches share state with.
  • vs IOS: classic IOS is one image in one shared memory space; a bug in one feature can crash the whole router, and state isn't kept across a process restart. IOS XE and NX-OS added process separation later; EOS was built around it from the start.

The EOS CLI (ch. 8): what's different from IOS

  • EOS is Linux (Fedora-based) with an IOS-like CLI on top, and one image for every platform. A new release can drop end-of-life ASICs and an old release can't drive new ones, so "one image" means any current release runs on any current switch. AW 8 p.39
  • Out of the box only SSH (and console) is on; telnet is off. username X secret Y is stored as SHA-512. There's no clear-text password and no service password-encryption. AW 8 p.39
  • An ambiguous abbreviation gives % Incomplete command; a typo gives a plain % Invalid input with no caret showing where. Use ? and Tab. AW 8 p.39–40
  • No do needed: run show and exec commands from any config mode. Typing a command from another mode at the same or a parent level switches modes for you (but not into a child mode). AW 8 p.40–41
  • Emacs keys work: Ctrl-A/E start/end of line, Ctrl-B/F back/forward a character, Esc-B/F back/forward a word. AW 8 p.40
  • Interfaces are all Ethernet regardless of speed: Ethernet1 (e1), slot/port on chassis (e3/1), and port/lane on multi-lane ports (e49/1, chassis e3/1/1). AW 8 p.40
  • No interface range: just list them, int e1-3,e10, and the prompt shows the set. $ means the last interface (e1-$); on boxes with laned ports you must give every form, e1-$/$. AW 8 p.40–41
  • Curly-brace expansion fills values per interface: {start,end[,step[,pad]]}. ip address 10.1.{1,4}.1/24 on e1–4 gives 10.1.1.1 to 10.1.4.1; description DC1-Row-{1,7,2,2} gives Row-01, Row-03… AW 8 p.40–41
  • show active = the config of the section you're in. show running-config all (or show run all int e24) includes defaults. AW 8 p.41–42

Pipes, filters and aliases

  • Pipe options: begin, exclude, include, section, json, no-more, nz (non-zero counters only), redirect/append/tee (to a URL), plus any Linux filter. AW 8 p.41, 44
  • Stacked includes don't work as expected; include is the CLI's own filter. Stacked greps do, because they're Linux. egrep -i, awk, tail, more and wc work too. AW 8 p.41–42
  • include and grep are case-sensitive and show only matching lines. section is case-insensitive and keeps the parent, so show run | section bgp shows the interface with a "BGP" description and the whole router bgp block. AW 8 p.44–45
  • Aliases save typing, and can take parameters (%1, %2) and run several lines. Example from the book: an alias that lists every interface's MAC with awk. AW 8 p.42
  • No paging on the console by default; add | more. show logging | tail works, and you can redirect to flash with a full Linux path (> /mnt/flash/x.txt). show logging follow streams the log. AW 8 p.42, 44

Configuration management (ch. 9)

  • Running vs startup: changes go live (into SysDB) at once; write memory (= copy running-config startup-config) makes them survive a reload. show running-config diffs shows what's unsaved.
  • Merge vs replace: the CLI is additive (you remove things with no). copy flash:file running-config merges a file in. configure replace flash:file replaces everything: anything not in the file goes back to default. The book replaced with a two-line file and lost the hostname and its SSH session. AW 9 p.45
  • Config sessions are the safe way: changes are staged, invisible to the switch until commit, and applied all at once. show session-config diffs (or show session-config named X diff) previews them; abort throws them away.
  • A session is a full copy of running-config taken when you start it, plus your edits, not a list of commands. Commit is a replace, so changes made to running-config after the session started are lost (the book's Ethernet6 description vanished). Start, edit and commit sessions quickly, and don't mix them with direct edits. AW 9 p.47–48
  • commit timer hh:mm:ss commits the session for that long. Unless you confirm with configure session NAME commit in time, EOS restores the automatic checkpoint it took at the timed commit. It's the modern reload in: same safety net, no reboot. show configuration sessions detail shows the time left. AW 9 p.48
  • Sessions live in RAM: lost on reboot. Defaults are 5 pending and 1 completed session kept (service configuration session max pending|completed). rollback clean-config inside a session starts from a factory-default config, which is how CloudVision builds a full intended config and pushes it as a replace. AW 9 p.48–49
  • configure replace swaps the whole running-config for a file or checkpoint with no reload. Sections that end up identical aren't touched, so BGP, MLAG or VXLAN keep running if their config didn't change. It's built on sessions, so it's all or nothing; check the file includes management, AAA and the mgmt VRF. Recover a bad one with configure replace flash:startup-config. AW 9 p.45; AW 7 p.37
  • Checkpoints are named saved copies of running-config. configure checkpoint save NAME, then configure checkpoint restore NAME or configure replace checkpoint:NAME (both replace, not merge). Kept in /mnt/flash/.checkpoints as plain text, so they survive reboots but use flash; 20 kept by default (service configuration checkpoint max saved). AW 9 p.45, 47
  • Backups and history: copy running-config flash:, scp: or tftp:; an on-startup-config event handler can copy each save off-box (3.1); CloudVision keeps every config version and can roll back (D2).
  • AAA basics: local users with roles, then TACACS+ or RADIUS for login, authorization and command accounting. Point the servers at the management VRF and source interface, and always keep local as the fallback.

Bash on EOS (ch. 12)

  • The switch is a real Linux server, not an emulation. bash from enable mode drops you into a shell as your EOS user (not root) in /home/<user>; use sudo when you really need root. Limit who gets bash with AAA command authorization. AW 12 p.55
  • Normal Linux tools work: ls, cat, grep, vmstat, ps, top, ifconfig/ip, tcpdump, python3. AW 12 p.55
  • CLI from bash: Cli opens an EOS CLI; Cli -c "show version" runs one command and prints it, so you can pipe or redirect it. Add -p 15 for privileged commands. FastCli is the old name; in current EOS Cli is the fast one. AW 12 p.55–56
  • Bash from the CLI: bash uname -a runs one Linux command without leaving the CLI; it runs from your home directory, so give full paths. AW 12 p.56
  • What survives a reboot: only /mnt/flash (flash:), /mnt/usb1, /mnt/drive (SSD, drive:, which also holds log archives) and /persist (→ /mnt/flash/persist). Your home directory, /tmp and package installs are gone. Keep scripts on flash. AW 12 p.56
  • Look around: /etc/Eos-release for the version (handy in scripts); pstree shows ProcMgr and every agent under it; ifconfig -s shows kernel names: et1 = Ethernet1, plus cpu, mirrorN (monitor sessions), vxlan, txfwd/txraw. AW 12 p.56–57
  • VRFs are Linux network namespaces (ns-MGMT). From bash, sudo ip netns exec ns-MGMT ping 10.0.0.5; from the CLI, cli vrf MGMT then bash puts your shell in that VRF. Without it, scripts use the default VRF and can't reach the management network.
  • Packet capture: tcpdump -i et1 (or tcpdump interface Ethernet1 from the CLI) sees only traffic to and from the CPU: control plane, punted and mirrored packets, not forwarded traffic. Write to flash with a size cap.
  • Scheduling: prefer the EOS schedule command (in the config, survives reboots, logs to flash) over cron, whose crontab lives outside flash. For event-driven scripts use event-handler (3.1). For structured data, call eAPI over the local Unix socket instead of scraping Cli -c text (7.3).

Know

  • Standard upgrade: copy the .swi to flash, check its hash, set boot system, save, reload. Outage equals reload time.

  • Plain reload prints MLAG ISSU warnings (STP not yet restartable, active-partial MLAGs, reload-delay below the recommended value, peer ports still errdisabled). Read them; reload now skips them. AW 18 p.97
  • Chassis with two supervisors: upgrade the standby, switch over (SSO), then the other.
  • CloudVision upgrades use an image bundle assigned to a container or device, which creates a task you run in a change control.

You opened a config session, then a colleague changed a port description directly. You commit your session. What happens to their change?

It's lost. The session is a copy of running-config from when it started; commit replaces running-config with it (AW 9 p.48). Check show session-config named X diff before committing.

The Stp agent crashed and restarted. Did STP reconverge, and how do you prove what happened?

No. The restarted agent read its state back from SysDB, so ports didn't go through discarding and learning. Prove it with show agent logs crash, show agent uptime (Stp younger than the switch), and AGENT-6-INITIALIZED in show logging with no STP state-change messages.

Why does show running-config change the moment you commit a config session, but not survive a reload?

Running-config is a rendering of SysDB in RAM. Commit writes the changes into SysDB; only write memory copies them to startup-config on flash, which is what SysDB is rebuilt from at boot.

You must change an uplink's VLAN list remotely and might lock yourself out. What do you type?

Use configure session NAME, make the change, check show session-config diffs, then commit timer 00:05:00. If you can still reach the switch, run configure session NAME commit to keep it. If not, it rolls back by itself.

leaf1 and leaf2 are an MLAG pair. In what order, and when, do you upgrade them?

Check ISSU compatibility. Upgrade one peer (often the secondary). Wait until show mlag is active/connected, all MLAG ports are active-full, and reload-delay has expired. Then upgrade the other peer. Hosts dual-homed by LACP stay up throughout.

A hotfix extension is installed but gone after a reload. Why?

It was never copied into boot-extensions. Run copy installed-extensions boot-extensions.

Know

  • Containers form a tree under Tenant. Configlets on a container apply to every device below it. Device configlets apply to one device.

ZTP in depth (AW ch. 13)

  • Trigger: a fixed switch boots into ZTP when startup-config is missing or zero bytes. It sets every Ethernet and Management port to no switchport and sends DHCP out of each connected one, retrying forever. AW 13 p.58
  • While ZTP runs, config you type is thrown away when it retries, and current EOS refuses write memory ("Cannot copy to startup-config when ZeroTouch is enabled"). Read the login banner. AW 13 p.58
  • zerotouch cancel reloads at once, no prompt, and skips ZTP for one boot only (it writes a disable-once flag to boot-config). Erase startup-config later and ZTP is back. AW 13 p.59
  • zerotouch disable also reloads without asking and turns ZTP off for good by writing DISABLE=True to flash:zerotouch-config. zerotouch enable is refused interactively; to turn it back on, delete flash:zerotouch-config (and startup-config) and reload. AW 13 p.59
  • Needs: a DHCP server, a file server (HTTP, often the same box), and a connected port. The DHCP reply's bootfile-name (option 67) is the URL to fetch. AW 13 p.58, 60
  • Config or script? If line one of the downloaded file is a shebang (#!/usr/bin/Cli, #!/usr/bin/env python3, #!/bin/bash), it's run as a script; otherwise it's applied as startup-config. Then the switch reloads. AW 13 p.60
  • A script can copy the startup-config and the right EOS image, then set boot system. No write memory is needed because the file was copied straight to startup-config. zerotouch script-exec-timeout limits how long a script may run. AW 13 p.59–60
  • At scale: CloudVision has a ZTP server built in (point DHCP at it); ztpserver (open source, EOS+ / arista-eosplus on GitHub) maps switches to configs by serial, MAC or LLDP neighbor; some sites auto-archive every config so a replacement switch rebuilds itself from the last copy. AW 13 p.61

ZTP with CloudVision, in practice

  • On-prem CVP: DHCP bootfile = http://<cvp>/ztp/bootstrap. The bootstrap script installs and starts TerminAttr pointed at CVP, the device registers, and it appears in Inventory (Undefined container, or ready to provision in Studios). You then assign configlets or Studio inputs and an image, and the change control pushes the full config (built with rollback clean-config sessions, AW 9 p.49).
  • CVaaS: download the bootstrap script from the CVaaS onboarding page; it carries an onboarding token, so host it on your own HTTP server and point DHCP at that.
  • ZTR: mark a failed device for replacement; the new one ZTPs and receives the old one's config and image.
  • Your CloudVision lab (EVPN VXLAN 101): watch for a factory-reset switch, its DHCP/bootfile, and where it lands in Inventory; then provision it into the EVPN fabric with a change control. This is the onboarding flow D2 tests.
  • DHCP options: 67 (bootfile-name) is what EOS uses and should be a full URL. 66 (TFTP server name) only matters for a TFTP bootfile. Arista sends a vendor class (option 60) and client identifier, so a DHCP server can match Arista switches, a model or a MAC and hand out different bootfiles; option 43 carries vendor-specific data some platforms use (EOS ZTP doesn't need it).
  • Security: an HTTP bootfile is unauthenticated, so anyone on the provisioning VLAN can hand a switch a config. Use HTTPS where your EOS and CloudVision support it, keep the ZTP VLAN isolated, give tokens a short life, and don't put secrets in bootstrap scripts.

ZTP troubleshooting and traps

  • Endless DHCP_QUERY_FAIL: no DHCP reply. Check the port and VLAN, the DHCP relay (ip helper-address) if the server is remote, and that the scope has free addresses. AW 13 p.58
  • Download fails: the URL is wrong, the web server is unreachable from that subnet, or the bootfile is an IP or path without http://.
  • Script applied as config (or the reverse): line one must be the shebang. A blank line or Windows line endings (CRLF) on line one break it. AW 13 p.60
  • Script fails: EXEC_SCRIPT error in the log. Test the script by hand in bash on a lab switch; check script-exec-timeout for large image copies.
  • Device never shows in CloudVision: TerminAttr not started, wrong address or VRF, an expired token, or clock skew breaking TLS (2.1).
  • Typing config on a ZTP switch "doesn't stick": ZTP is still on. Cancel, disable, or let it finish. AW 13 p.58
  • Lab vEOS/cEOS sitting in ZTP: zerotouch disable, or give it a startup-config (containerlab does).

Arista Warrior ch. 23, LANZ: notes by page

  • p.123 show interfaces has no buffer information because buffer state changes faster than you can read it. Throughput from SNMP is the difference between two octet counters divided by the poll interval, so a calm 5-minute average can hide a second full of bursts over line rate. LANZ reads queue depth from the ASIC at sub-millisecond granularity, on supported hardware only.
  • p.123–124 Model a port buffer as a tank filled faster than it drains. LANZ uses a high and a low queue threshold: crossing the high mark logs a Start (S) record, Update (U) records follow while it stays congested, and dropping under the low mark logs an End (E) record. LANZ watches the buffer; it doesn't do flow control.
  • p.124 Two modes. Polling (P records, roughly "was there congestion in the last second") is the default on some platforms such as the 7280R. Notifying mode gives the detailed S/U/E records but uses more hardware resources.
  • p.124–125 The book's lab makes congestion on demand: a static-route loop between two switches, one port shaped down to 5 Gbps with shape rate, then a burst of UDP from iperf in bash. load-interval 1 makes the interface rates readable second by second.
  • p.125 Enable LANZ globally with queue-monitor length, then set per-interface thresholds (high then low). show queue-monitor length status shows the mode and every port's thresholds. show queue-monitor length <intf> shows the records with queue length and duration.
  • p.125 The Ingress Port-set column lists every port on the same ASIC core as the congested port, so it narrows down where the burst came in, not the exact port. show platform jericho mapping shows the port-to-core mapping on Jericho-based switches.
  • p.126 For analysis, CloudVision graphs LANZ data, and it can be streamed out (Google Protocol Buffers). Without those, the csv keyword dumps up to the last 100,000 over-threshold events, oldest first; redirect it to a file on flash: and open it in a spreadsheet.

Layer 2 and Layer 3 Designs

Two fabric shapes. L2LS keeps the spines as a Layer 2/3 boundary with MLAG, so STP sees no loops. L3LS routes every leaf-to-spine link, uses ECMP, and carries Layer 2 over VXLAN where it's needed.

4.1

L2LS: VLANs, STP, LACP, MLAG, VARP/VRRP

Know

  • EOS default STP is MSTP. Keep the spines (MLAG pair) as root; edge ports get portfast and BPDU guard.
  • MLAG makes two switches look like one LACP partner: both send the same chassis ID, so the downstream device just sees a normal port-channel and knows nothing about MLAG. AW 18 p.89, 91
  • The peer link is an ordinary trunk port-channel (use at least two members). VLAN 4094 goes in a trunk group so it rides only the peer link, and its SVI gets no autostate so it stays up. AW 18 p.90
  • Domain ID is case-sensitive and must match on both peers. Each peer points peer-address at the other's VLAN 4094 IP. AW 18 p.90–91
  • Every MLAG member must be a port-channel on each peer, even with one link per peer. The mlag number links them; keep the port-channel numbers the same too, for your own sanity. AW 18 p.92
  • reload-delay keeps ports errdisabled after a reboot (all but L3 and peer-link ports) until the peer has synced. reload-delay non-mlag lets routed ports come up first so routing settles before MLAGs rehash. AW 18 p.94
  • Dual-primary (split brain): a heartbeat to the peer's management IP. If the peer link dies but the heartbeat still answers, the secondary errdisables its ports after the delay. AW 18 p.94, 96
  • VARP: both MLAG peers answer ARP for the same virtual IP with one global virtual MAC, and both route, so traffic is routed on whichever switch receives it and never crosses the peer link. No protocol, no timers, so failover has no lag. AW 19 p.103–104
  • VRRP: one master routes, backups listen; only the master sends advertisements (every 1 s by default). Use it where VARP can't go, such as routed Ethernet ports. AW 19 p.99–100, 104
  • Routed subinterfaces (Ethernet1.100) give one VLAN-tagged L3 link per VRF on a routed port.

Configure (leaf1)

Arista Warrior ch. 19, First-Hop Redundancy: notes by page

  • p.99–100 VRRP is the open standard (RFCs 2338, 3768, 5798) and works like HSRP. A virtual router is a VRID (1–255) plus its virtual IPs. The same VRID can be reused on different interfaces, but not the same VRID and VIP pair. show vrrp shows nothing until ip routing is on.
  • p.99–100 Defaults: advertisements every 1 s, preemption on, priority 100. The virtual MAC is 0000.5e00.01xx where xx is the VRID in hex.
  • p.101 Preemption can be turned off per group, or delayed: a minimum delay before taking over, and a separate delay after a reload. Advertisement timers can be changed, and default puts each setting back.
  • p.101 Priority runs 1–254, higher wins. If a master already exists with the same priority, a new router stays backup. Raise priority to make a router master.
  • p.102 With preemption on, the higher-priority router takes master back as soon as it returns. EOS won't accept a VIP that equals an interface's own IP.
  • p.102 A group can hold several VIPs: add extras as secondary. Authentication can be clear text (visible in show output) or MD5 (hidden in the running config). Configure the same on every router in the group.
  • p.103 Tracking: create a track object on an interface's line protocol, then have the VRRP group either decrement its priority or shut down when it goes down. A decrement must drop the master below the backup's priority to cause a failover.
  • p.103 A VRRP group can be shut down on its own. show vrrp brief is the summary view in busy configs. show track lists objects and what tracks them; spell out brief, because a shortened form is read as an object name.
  • p.103–104 VARP (L3 anycast gateway): every switch answers ARP and gratuitous ARP for the shared VIP with one virtual MAC. Hosts use whichever reply they get first, so there's no real load balancing, but with MLAG traffic is routed locally on both switches and stays off the peer link.
  • p.104 VARP setup is two lines: the global virtual-router MAC (same on both switches, and it must have the locally administered bit set; 00:1c:73:00:00:99 is the usual one), then the VIP under each SVI. It works only on VLAN interfaces. You can ping the VIP. Several VIPs per VLAN are allowed. The VIP is never used as a source address except for gratuitous ARP.

L3LS architecture and underlay choices

Know

  • Every leaf connects to every spine with a routed /31. No STP in the fabric; all uplinks forward through ECMP.
  • Sizing: the number of spines sets each leaf's ECMP width and uplink count; spine port count sets the maximum number of leaves; leaf downlink vs uplink bandwidth is the oversubscription ratio. See Hardware foundations. AW 5 p.26–27
  • The underlay only needs to carry loopbacks (router IDs and VTEP addresses). Tenant routes live in the overlay.
  • Underlay options: eBGP (one protocol everywhere, clear failure domains, RFC 7938), OSPF or IS-IS (simple, then iBGP or eBGP for the overlay).
  • Common ASN plan: all spines share one ASN; each leaf (or MLAG pair) gets its own. Spines never peer with each other.
  • VXLAN gives Layer 2 across the routed fabric. EVPN is the control plane that tells VTEPs about MACs, IPs and floods.
  • Scale out by adding spines (bandwidth) or leaves (ports). A 5-stage design adds super-spines for multiple pods.

Design best practice: MTU, BGP design, validation

Know

  • VXLAN adds 50 bytes (outer Ethernet 14 + IP 20 + UDP 8 + VXLAN 8). Set fabric links to 9214 so 9000-byte host frames still fit.
  • MLAG peer link and the iBGP SVI need jumbo MTU too.
  • eBGP in the underlay and overlay is the common Arista design. iBGP overlay with route reflectors on the spines is the alternative.
  • If leaves reuse ASNs across racks, they need allowas-in. Unique ASNs avoid that.
  • Validation habit: after every change, check neighbors, routes, and a ping with a large, unfragmentable packet.

Practice: D4

show mlag says "inconsistent". Where do you look first?

show mlag config-sanity. It lists the global and per-interface settings that differ between peers (VLAN lists, STP, port-channel options).

leaf1 was MLAG primary, reloaded, and came back. Which is primary now, and are leaf1's host ports up?

leaf2 stays primary; MLAG doesn't preempt. leaf1's ports (except routed ports and the peer link) stay errdisabled until reload-delay expires; show mlag detail shows the time left. (AW ch.18 p.94)

You set spanning-tree priority 4096 on leaf2 and nothing changed. Why, and why is that dangerous?

leaf2 is MLAG secondary, and only the primary runs STP. The change is saved and takes effect when leaf2 becomes primary, perhaps months later at a failover. config-sanity won't flag it. Put identical STP config on both peers. (AW ch.18 p.97–98)

The peer link fails but both switches are up. What stops both becoming primary and what happens next?

Dual-primary detection: the secondary still hears the primary's heartbeat over management, so after the configured delay it errdisables its interfaces. Without a heartbeat, both go primary (split brain). (AW ch.18 p.94, p.96)

You configured ip virtual-router address 10.10.10.1 on Vlan10 on both leaves, but hosts can't reach their gateway. What's missing?

The global ip virtual-router mac-address, the same on both leaves. Without it show ip virtual-router reports the MAC isn't configured and nothing answers ARP for the VIP. (AW ch.19 p.104)

VRRP master has priority 105, backup 100. You track the master's uplink with decrement 5. The uplink fails. Does the backup take over?

No. 105 − 5 = 100 ties with the backup, and an equal priority doesn't displace a working master. Decrement by more than the gap (for example 10). (AW ch.19 p.101, p.103)

A 9000-byte ping between hosts on different leaves fails; 1400 works. What's the likely cause?

An underlay link with MTU below 9050. VXLAN adds 50 bytes. Set fabric links to 9214 and re-test with size 8972 df-bit.

Why do spines in an eBGP L3LS usually share one ASN?

A route learned from one spine can't be re-advertised by a leaf to another spine and accepted (same ASN in the path), which stops leaf-to-spine-to-leaf-to-spine path hunting, and the config stays uniform.

Overlay Protocols

The center of the Engineering exam. VXLAN is the data plane: a 24-bit VNI in a UDP header, between VTEP loopbacks. EVPN is the control plane: BGP routes that tell VTEPs which MACs and IPs sit behind which VTEP, and who wants floods. Read R1 after this domain.

6.1

VXLAN: VTEPs, VNIs, HER, VXLAN with MLAG

Know

  • VTEP = the Vxlan1 interface, sourced from a loopback. UDP port 4789. The VNI is 24 bits (about 16.7 million segments) against 12 bits for a VLAN, so different tenants can reuse a VLAN number on different VNIs. AW 21 p.110–111, 113
  • VXLAN is stateless encapsulation, not a tunnel that comes "up": a VTEP wraps the frame in UDP/IP and sends it to the remote VTEP's IP. Nothing is up or down until traffic flows. AW 21 p.112
  • L2 VNI maps a VLAN to a VNI (bridging). L3 VNI maps a VRF to a VNI (routing between VTEPs in symmetric IRB).
  • BUM (broadcast, unknown unicast, multicast) handling: HER sends a copy to each VTEP in the flood list. The control plane decides how that list is built: manual, CVX, or EVPN type-3 routes. A multicast underlay is the other option, but VXLAN with MLAG and VXLAN routing need HER or EVPN. AW 21 p.112–114
  • Flood lists: a default list (vxlan flood vtep) applies to every mapped VLAN; a per-VLAN list (vxlan vlan 10 flood vtep) overrides it for that VLAN. AW 21 p.114–115
  • MLAG pair = one logical VTEP. Both peers use the same Loopback1 IP and identical VLAN-to-VNI maps and flood lists; Loopback0 stays unique. Add a routed link between the peers so a peer that loses its uplink still reaches the fabric. AW 21 p.118–119
  • Data-plane learning: without EVPN, VTEPs learn remote MACs from received frames.

6.2

EVPN overlay: route types, VRFs, IRB

Know

  • Overlay sessions run between Loopback0 addresses (eBGP multihop to the spines, or iBGP to route reflectors), address family evpn, with extended communities.
  • Spines in an eBGP overlay keep the original next hop: next-hop-unchanged. Otherwise traffic tunnels to the spine.
  • Type 2 MAC/IP: a host's MAC (and IP) behind a VTEP. Type 3 IMET: "send me floods for this VNI", builds the flood list. Type 5 IP prefix: subnets and external routes in a VRF.
  • RD makes a route unique; RT decides which MAC-VRF or IP-VRF imports it. AVD convention: RD = router-id:VNI, RT = VNI:VNI.
  • VLAN-based service: one EVI per VLAN. VLAN-aware bundle: one EVI with many VLANs, one RD/RT, Ethernet tag = VLAN.
  • Symmetric IRB: route at ingress and egress through the L3 VNI. Leaves only need the VLANs they host. Scales well. Asymmetric IRB: ingress routes and bridges into the destination VNI. Every leaf needs every VLAN; no L3 VNI.
  • Anycast gateway: ip address virtual with the same IP and virtual MAC on every leaf.
  • L2-only VLAN (30 here): VLAN plus VNI plus MAC-VRF, no SVI, nothing in the VRF.

6.3

EVPN multihoming (ESI, all-active)

Know

  • The standards-based alternative to MLAG: two (or more) leaves share an Ethernet Segment, identified by a 10-byte ESI, with no peer link.
  • Type 1 Ethernet A-D: per-ES (fast mass withdraw when the segment fails) and per-EVI (aliasing, so remote VTEPs load-balance to all leaves on the segment).
  • Type 4 Ethernet Segment: discovers the peers on an ES and elects the designated forwarder (DF), which alone forwards BUM to the host.
  • Both leaves must present the same LACP system ID so the host bundles them into one LAG.
  • DF election defaults to modulus (by VLAN). Preference-based election lets you pick the DF.

      route-target import 03:03:02:02:01:01
   lacp system-id 0303.0202.0101
interface Ethernet5
   channel-group 5 mode active

! optional: choose the DF
   evpn ethernet-segment
      designated-forwarder election algorithm preference 200

host1 (VLAN 10, leaf1) pings host2 (VLAN 10, leaf3) fine but can't ping 10.20.20.11 (VLAN 20, leaf3). What do you check?

Bridging works, routing doesn't, so check the L3 parts: vxlan vrf TENANT-A vni 50001 on both, the VRF RD/RTs in BGP, ip virtual-router mac-address, and show ip route vrf TENANT-A on leaf1 for a type-2 host route or type-5 subnet pointing at leaf3's VTEP.

leaf1's VLAN 10 flood list holds 10.255.1.5. You type "vxlan flood vtep 10.255.1.7". What's in the list now?

Only 10.255.1.7. Without add the command replaces the list. Use vxlan flood vtep add 10.255.1.7. (AW ch.21 p.114)

Right after configuring static HER, show vxlan vtep is empty. Is VXLAN broken?

Not necessarily. That command lists VTEPs that have exchanged traffic recently. Send traffic (ping between hosts), then check again, and confirm the configured list with show vxlan flood vtep. (AW ch.21 p.116)

Which EVPN route type tells a VTEP to add another VTEP to its flood list?

Type 3, Inclusive Multicast Ethernet Tag (IMET).

Leaves receive EVPN routes but import none of them. The first thing to check?

Route targets. Make sure extended communities are sent (send-community or send-community extended) on every hop, and that import RTs match export RTs.

Symmetric or asymmetric IRB for 200 VLANs across 40 leaves where each rack uses 10 VLANs?

Symmetric. Each leaf only configures its own 10 VLANs plus the L3 VNI. Asymmetric would need all 200 VLANs and SVIs on every leaf.

7.3

AVD, ANTA and eAPI: the fabric as code

Not named on the exam datasheet. It's here because it's how the same fabric is built in production, and because older "ACE L5" meant the automation track.

Know

  • AVD is an Ansible collection (and the Python library PyAVD). eos_designs turns a few YAML files describing the fabric into per-device structured config; eos_cli_config_gen renders EOS config and docs from that; cv_deploy pushes through CloudVision (or eos_config_deploy_eapi direct).
  • ANTA tests the live network (BGP up, routes present, MLAG healthy). AVD's eos_validate_state generates ANTA tests from the design.
  • eAPI is JSON-RPC over HTTPS: send CLI commands, get structured JSON back. Everything else (AVD, CloudVision, ANTA) uses it or gNMI.
  • eAPI can also listen only on localhost, so scripts run on the switch itself (from bash) can drive the CLI without exposing the API on the network. The FlexRoute chapter uses this to bulk-load routes. AW 20 p.106
  • Why eAPI: expect scripts break when a prompt differs, and screen scraping breaks when the output format changes. | json on the CLI and eAPI return named keys instead, so the script doesn't care about layout. AW 30 p.162–164
  • runCmds takes a list of commands and returns a list with one dictionary per command, in the same order: result[0]["version"] is the version from the first command. Key names are camelCase and case-sensitive. AW 30 p.170
  • Each request runs at privilege 1 unless the list starts with enable. With an enable secret, send {"cmd": "enable", "input": "<secret>"}. Config is just more list items: configure, interface Ethernet5, description …; config commands return empty dicts. AW 30 p.171–172
  • Nothing interactive: no top, no watch, no "are you sure?" prompts. Use reload now, not reload. AW 30 p.172
  • "format": "text" returns the CLI text instead, still one dict per command, under result[i]["output"]. Handy for saving show running-config to a file. AW 30 p.172
  • The /explorer page on the switch builds and sends real requests (so config you send applies), shows the JSON, has a JavaScript script editor and the full command docs. Scripts post to /command-api. AW 30 p.167–168
  • The lab topology for this guide is cEOS in containerlab, and a containerlab copy of production is the usual way to test AVD changes before they ship. See R5 cEOS, vEOS and VMs. AW 31
  • Inventory groups map to roles: FABRIC > DC1 > DC1_SPINES, DC1_L3_LEAVES, plus NETWORK_SERVICES and CONNECTED_ENDPOINTS.

EOS gotchas for Cisco people

Where IOS habits fail on EOS. Most are quick exam points: a config that "looks right" but doesn't work. Based on Arista Warrior ch. 33, "Aristacisms" (pp.188, 190–193), checked against current EOS syntax.

speed 1000 + duplex fullThere is no duplex command, and speed 1000 is rejected. Speed and duplex are one keyword. AW 33 p.188speed 1000full (older syntax: speed forced 1000full) or speed auto. Fix hard-coded ports before pasting IOS configs.

Arista Warrior ch. 31 (pp.175–180) and ch. 32 (pp.181, 184–187) cover both directions: EOS running inside a container or VM (cEOS, vEOS), and containers or VMs running on an EOS switch.

cEOS: EOS in a container

  • A container shares the host's kernel and packages one application with its dependencies, so it starts in seconds and uses far less memory than a VM (vEOS). AW 31 p.175
  • Why Arista built it: to run EOS as the NOS on white-box switches with merchant silicon, managed by the same orchestrators the cloud titans use for their other containers. Labs are a side benefit. AW 31 p.175
  • Get the cEOS-lab image from the Arista software downloads page (account needed) and docker import it with a tag. Run it with docker create … /sbin/init, attach networks, docker start. AW 31 p.175–176
  • Get a CLI with docker exec -it <name> Cli. docker attach only shows the boot console. Interfaces come up as Ethernet1… with a software data plane. AW 31 p.176–177
# what the book does by hand (4.21); containerlab does this for you
docker import cEOS-lab.tar.xz ceos:4.21.1F
docker create --name ceos1 --privileged \
  -e CEOS=1 -e EOS_PLATFORM=ceoslab -e container=docker \
  -e ETBA=1 -e INTFTYPE=eth \
  -e SKIP_ZEROTOUCH_BARRIER_IN_SYSDBINIT=1 \
  -it ceos:4.21.1F /sbin/init
docker network create net-1
docker network connect net-1 ceos1   # becomes Ethernet1
docker start ceos1
docker exec -it ceos1 Cli

cEOS gotchas and how containerlab fixes them

  • No LLDP across Docker networks. Docker networks are Linux bridges, and bridges swallow link-local multicast like LLDP. Ping works; show lldp neighbors is empty. The fix is veth pairs (point-to-point links), which is what containerlab builds. AW 31 p.177
  • Interface order isn't guaranteed past three networks. Docker may attach net-4 before net-3, so Ethernet numbers don't match your diagram. containerlab names each link end eth1, eth2 … explicitly, so eth3 is always Ethernet3. AW 31 p.177
  • Long docker create lines in scripts: a space after a \ breaks the continuation, and you get "-e: command not found". In vi, :set list shows trailing whitespace. AW 31 p.176
  • Hostnames and config are lost if you remove the container. containerlab saves startup configs per node (containerlab save) and can start nodes from your own config files.
  • Not everything is emulated: no real ASIC means no hardware TCAM limits, no LANZ, no optics, and some features differ from hardware. Fine for control plane, config and automation testing; not for performance.

name: two-ceos

  nodes:
    ceos1: { kind: ceos }
    ceos2: { kind: ceos }

Containers on an EOS switch

  • EOS includes Docker; in bash it needs sudo. But manage containers from the EOS CLI with container-manager, so they live in the running-config and CloudVision sees them. AW 31 p.177–178
  • Image vs container: an image is what you pull (like a disk image); a container is a running instance of it (like a VM). Each bash docker run -it ubuntu makes a new container with a random name. AW 31 p.179
  • A container stops when its command ends. The book's Ubuntu container shows "exited" because /bin/bash with no terminal returns at once. A real service runs a long-lived daemon. AW 31 p.178–179
  • Reboots: only flash: and drive: survive, so at boot on-boot containers pull their image again from the internet. container-manager backup container X saves it to /mnt/flash/.containermgr/X.tar so the switch uses that instead. The book's Ubuntu backup was about 90 MB: watch flash. AW 31 p.179–180

vEOS: EOS as a virtual machine

Know

  • vEOS is the same EOS code compiled for a VM, not a separate product. What it can't do is anything that needs an ASIC: TapAgg, LANZ, FlexRoute, Direct Flow, hardware TCAM limits. Software features all work: LLDP, MLAG, ZTP, STP, routing, bash, Python, eAPI, event manager, scheduler, event monitor. That makes it a control-plane and interoperability test tool. AW 32 p.181, 187
  • vEOS-lab: free with an arista.com login, for labs. vEOS-router (now usually called CloudEOS): for AWS, Azure and GCP, licensed, with a faster forwarding path and IPsec VPN. AW 32 p.181
  • Newer releases ship Aboot and vEOS as one combined image, which makes building VMs simpler. AW 32 p.181
  • vEOS vs cEOS for labs: vEOS boots slower and needs more RAM per node (about 2 GB), but behaves more like a real switch (its own kernel, Aboot, Management1). cEOS is lighter and starts in seconds. Both are fine for this exam's topics.

vEOS in EVE-NG (your setup)

  • The book builds its lab in VirtualBox (pp.182–183, skipped here). In EVE-NG, vEOS-lab is a QEMU node: convert the .vmdk to qcow2, put it in a veos-<version> folder under the EVE-NG QEMU images directory, and fix permissions. Check EVE-NG's own vEOS how-to for the exact folder and file name for your EVE-NG version.
  • Give each node 2 GB RAM or more. Put Management1 on an EVE-NG cloud/bridge network that your Ubuntu automation server can reach, so Ansible and AVD can talk eAPI to every node.
  • EVE-NG links are point-to-point, so LLDP works between nodes, unlike plain Docker bridges.

VMs running on an EOS switch

  • EOS can host VMs with virtual-machine. You can; that doesn't mean you should. A VM eats RAM, and running out of memory is one of the worst things that can happen to a switch. AW 32 p.184
  • Default memory is a tiny 128 MB; the maximum on 4.21 was 2047 MB (not 2048). Many switches only have 4 GB in total. AW 32 p.184
  • NICs bind to Management1 or to VLAN SVIs. The SVI must exist (it needn't have an IP), or the line is ignored with "Device Vlan101 does not exist". EOS assigns the MACs. AW 32 p.184
  • Each VM needs its own copy of the disk image; two VMs on one .vmdk will fight. Copies are about 700 MB each on flash. AW 32 p.184–185, 187
  • Inside a vEOS guest, data ports didn't pass traffic until the IP was also put on the matching vmnicet bridge interface in bash. The book's workaround from Arista developers; treat it as a 4.21-era quirk. AW 32 p.185
  • A guest can reach other hosts on the network and other guests, but not the host switch's own IPs. The VLAN in the guest is local and untagged, so its number needn't match the host's. You can't nest a VM inside a vEOS guest. AW 32 p.185–186

VM-on-switch traps

  • Ctrl-C in the VM console exits the console, not just config mode. Use Ctrl-Z or end inside the guest. AW 32 p.185
  • A VM left enabled and saved starts again on every reload and takes its RAM back. Disable it when done; the disk image still sits on flash. AW 32 p.186
  • Two 2 GB VMs used 4 GB, the whole RAM of many switches. Never do this on a production box during the day. If you must, use a switch with lots of RAM and an SSD (drive:) for the images, not flash. AW 32 p.185, 187
  • Guests come up slowly and may sit in ZTP: run zerotouch disable inside the guest. AW 32 p.184

Risks of running containers on switches

  • Resources: CPU and memory are shared with routing agents. A runaway container can starve BGP. Limit it with Docker options, and watch show processes top once.
  • Flash: images and backups are tens to hundreds of MB, and flash is small. Keep an eye on it before upgrades, which need room for the new EOS image.
  • Support: TAC supports EOS, not your container. Expect to reproduce problems without it.
  • Security: a container is new code with network access on a core device. Use trusted images from a private registry, least privilege, and patch it like a server.
  • Lifecycle: EOS upgrades can change Docker or container-manager behavior. Test the container on the new release in a lab first, which is a cEOS use case in itself.
